**DeFi Onboarding & Growth Playbook**

**Chapter 1 — Growth Principles**

**1.0 The central question**

Traditional growth marketing often asks:

**How do we acquire more users?**

For DeFi, that’s incomplete.

We should ask:

**How do we turn a person who is merely aware of a product into a user who repeatedly performs economically meaningful actions—and eventually helps bring in other users?**

That gives us a chain:

**Awareness → Acquisition → Onboarding → Activation → Retention → Referral → Economic Value**

But there is an important distinction:

**This is not necessarily a linear funnel.**

The ultimate objective is to turn it into a **growth loop**.

⸻

**1.1 Funnel vs. Growth Loop**

**Traditional funnel**

Advertisement

     ↓

Website

     ↓

Signup

     ↓

First transaction

     ↓

Repeat customer

The company continually has to buy or generate new traffic.

**Growth loop**

```
       ┌──────────────┐

             ↓              │

          New User          │

             ↓              │

       First Transaction   │

             ↓              │

          Receives Value    │

             ↓              │

        Shares / Refers ────┘
```

Now the user becomes part of the acquisition mechanism.

This is one of the things I want us to look for in every case study:

**Did the product merely acquire users, or did it cause users to help acquire additional users?**

  

⸻

  

**1.2 Awareness isn’t acquisition**

This distinction sounds obvious, but it becomes extremely important in crypto.

Someone can:
- see a YouTube video
- follow a Twitter account
- join Discord
- read an article
- hear about a token
- visit a website

without ever becoming a customer.

So:
**Awareness ≠ Acquisition**

Acquisition should mean that the person takes an action that establishes a relationship with the product.

For example:

**Robinhood**

→ downloads app  
→ opens account

**DeFi**

→ connects wallet  
→ creates/deposits into a position

⸻

**1.3 Acquisition isn’t activation**

This is probably the **single most important distinction for our research**.

Imagine a DeFi protocol reports:

“We acquired 500,000 new wallets.”

That sounds impressive.

But what if:

- 400,000 never transact
- 75,000 transact once
- 20,000 farm an incentive
- 5,000 become recurring users

The headline number tells us very little.

So we’ll define:

**Acquisition**

**The person becomes a user.**

**Activation**

**The person experiences the product’s core value.**

For a DEX:
Connect wallet → make first swap.

For lending:
Deposit collateral → borrow.

For a payment system:
Make first payment.

For Robinhood:
Fund account → make first investment.

⸻

**1.4 “First meaningful action”**

I propose that the playbook use this concept extensively.

We’ll call it:

**FMA — First Meaningful Action**

The FMA is the first action that demonstrates that the user understands enough about the product to extract value from it.

That allows us to compare very different platforms.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Platform**|**Possible FMA**|
|Robinhood|First investment|
|Coinbase|First purchase|
|Uniswap|First swap|
|Aave|First deposit/borrow|
|dYdX|First trade|
|NFT marketplace|First purchase/listing|
|Cardano DeFi|First successful DeFi transaction|

This gives us a much better metric than downloads or wallet connections. 

⸻

**1.5 Education shouldn’t necessarily precede action**

This is where Web3 gets interesting.

Traditional education often looks like:
**Learn → understand → use**

But some of the best onboarding systems invert it:
**Try → experience → understand → advance**

For example:
“Swap $5.”

Then:
**You just completed your first decentralized exchange transaction. Here’s what happened.**

That’s **contextual education**.
The user doesn’t need to understand an entire financial system before experiencing it.

This gives us four education models:

**Passive**
Articles, videos, newsletters.

**Contextual**
Information appears when the user encounters a concept.

**Interactive**
Quizzes, simulations and challenges.

**Experiential**
**Learn by performing the actual action.**

That fourth category will become particularly important when we examine **Optimism, Galxe and RabbitHole**.

⸻

**1.6 Incentives are not all equal**

We should resist the temptation to simply record:

“Platform X gave users rewards.”

Instead, we’ll ask:

**What behavior was being incentivized?**

Consider four possibilities.

**A. Registration**
Create account → receive $10.

**B. Activation**
Make first trade → receive $10.

**C. Useful activity**
Trade $1,000 → receive reward.

**D. Valuable network contribution**
Provide liquidity → receive reward.

These have radically different economic implications. And this leads directly into something I think should become a core principle of the playbook:

**The closer an incentive is to creating genuine product value, the more interesting it is.**

⸻

**1.7 Incentive sustainability**

This is where we connect growth hacking to your broader blockchain research.

Suppose a protocol gives:

**$1 million in incentives**

and gets:

**$100 million in trading volume.**

That sounds fantastic.

But we need to ask:
- How much revenue did the activity generate?
- How much of the activity was organic?
- How much disappeared when rewards ended?
- Did users remain?
- Did liquidity remain?
- Did users acquire other users?
- Did the protocol create an enduring economic relationship?

The critical distinction is:

**Subsidized growth**
Users participate because they’re being paid.

versus

**Product-market growth**
Users participate because the product provides something they genuinely value.

The best systems may combine the two:

**Incentive gets the user through the door.**

**Product value gives them a reason to stay.**

⸻

**1.8 Referral mechanics**

We’ll treat referrals as a distinct growth mechanism. There are at least five models worth studying.

**1. Simple**
Refer friend → get $10.

**2. Double-sided**
Refer friend → both users receive reward.

**3. Progressive**
1 referral → reward  
5 referrals → larger reward  
25 referrals → larger reward

**4. Affiliate**
Your referrals generate continuing revenue.

**5. Network**
Your economic reward depends on activity generated by your network.

The fifth is particularly interesting for Web3—but also potentially much more complex from an economic and regulatory perspective.

⸻

**1.9 Gamification**

We shouldn’t equate gamification with games. A progress bar can be gamification.

So can:
- points
- levels
- badges
- streaks
- milestones
- quests
- leaderboards
- collectibles
- unlockable features
- status

The important question is:
**What behavior does the game mechanic reinforce?**

A leaderboard for meaningless clicks is not useful growth.

A progression system that teaches someone how to use increasingly sophisticated financial products could be extremely powerful.

⸻

**1.10 Retention**

Acquisition gets someone **in the door**.

Retention answers:

**Why do they come back tomorrow?**

We’ll categorize retention mechanisms as:

**Utility**
“I need this.”

**Economic**
“I make/save money using this.”

**Habit**
“I check/use it every day.”

**Information**
“I want to see what’s happening.”

**Community**
“My social network is here.”

**Progression**
“I’ve built something and don’t want to lose my progress.”

**Ownership**
“I have assets/reputation/status here.”

**Rewards**
“I’ll lose an economic benefit if I stop.”

Again, we’ll distinguish **healthy retention** from retention that’s entirely subsidy-dependent.

⸻ 

**1.11 The economic loop**

This is where I think our playbook becomes different from a conventional Web3 growth guide.

Ultimately:

User

 ↓

Action

 ↓

Value created

 ↓

Protocol revenue / network utility

 ↓

Resources available for growth

 ↓

More users

That’s the **self-sustaining growth loop**.

Compare that with:

Token treasury

 ↓

Rewards

 ↓

Users

 ↓

Farm rewards

 ↓

Sell token

 ↓

Users leave

 ↓

More token rewards required

Both can produce impressive user numbers.

They are **very different growth systems**.

⸻

**1.12 Our master growth equation**

I think we should eventually be able to reduce every case study to something like:

**Acquisition × Activation × Retention × Referral × Economic Value**

The exact mathematics can come later, but conceptually:

**A growth system isn’t strong merely because it acquires lots of people.**

It becomes powerful when:

**more users → more meaningful activity → more value → more resources → more users**

without requiring proportionally increasing subsidies.

⸻

**1.13 The principle I want to carry into every case study**

**Reduce friction before increasing incentives.**

This is especially relevant to the Cardano thesis. If a user wants to do something but the onboarding process is unnecessarily difficult:

**Reducing friction creates permanent value.**

If instead we compensate the user for tolerating the friction:

**we may simply be renting users.**

That gives us an important hierarchy to investigate:

**1. Remove friction**

↓

**2. Make the value proposition obvious**

↓

**3. Educate at the moment of need**

↓

**4. Incentivize initial behavior**

↓

**5. Create progression**

↓

**6. Create retention**

↓

**7. Create referrals**

↓

**8. Turn users into an acquisition channel**

And then the ultimate question:

**Can the resulting economic activity sustain the growth system?**
